# JeffCrunch

JeffCrunch is the spreadsheet app of JeffOffice. JeffOffice is an independent project: JeffCrunch aims to work with Excel workbooks and the people who use them (compatibility and interoperability), and then to do more, and do it better. Its feature checklist is [features/jeffcrunch.md](features/jeffcrunch.md); this note is the design, the order it is built in, and how each step is measured.

## The decisions (October 22, 2026)

- **Our own calculation engine**, in C++ (`libs/base/calc`). A spreadsheet engine is not a part you call once: every cell read, edit, recalculation, and undo goes through it, so it has to own its storage, recalculate only what an edit changed, and share undo with the app. It also serves JeffWrite's fields, JeffBase, chart axes, and `TEXT()`. IronCalc, an open-source engine, is run once through the same yardstick when the yardstick exists, so the choice is scored rather than argued.
- **About 110 functions first.** In the public workbooks, the 50 most used functions cover 91% of the workbooks that use functions and the 100 most used cover 99%. A formula with a function JeffCrunch doesn't have yet keeps the result the file stored, and shows `#NAME?` only once something it depends on changes.
- **Macros are kept, never run.** A workbook's macro project is read, kept untouched, and saved back. Converting macros to a JeffOffice script (translated to JavaScript, reviewed by you, run only when you allow it) comes after the preview, as its own project.
- **Formats.** `.xlsx` is the default. The first release opens `.xls` (BIFF5 to BIFF8, the files of 1993 to 2003), `.ods`, and `.csv`; a workbook opened from `.xls` is saved as `.xlsx` under a new name (the `.xls` is never written), and saving `.xls` itself and `.ods` follow once `.xlsx` is solid. Whatever JeffCrunch can't edit yet is kept and written back as it was.

## The preview bar

JeffCrunch ships as a preview when, on public workbooks never used for tuning, it meets every line below. Each is measured by a program, with no other spreadsheet involved: a workbook stores the last result of every formula, so recalculating every formula and comparing with what the file stored checks the engine against thousands of real answers.

| Measure | Preview bar |
|---|---|
| Opening | Every file opens with no crash or hang; 99% of undamaged files keep their own cell count |
| Recalculating every formula against its stored result (to 15 digits; text, true/false, and error kind exactly; volatile, external, and unsupported formulas listed apart, and so are workbooks that say they were saved before their calculation finished, whose stored results are not their formulas' answers) | 90% of formula workbooks match fully; 98% of formula cells match; each of the 50 most used functions matches 99% of the time |
| Function test files from the public LibreOffice test suite | All for the first 110 functions; 80% of the rest |
| Number formats, checked against stored `TEXT()`, `FIXED`, and `DOLLAR` results | 98% |
| Saving | No crashes; 99.5% of workbooks read back the same; 100% of parts JeffCrunch didn't touch written back identical |
| `.xls` | Every file opens; 98% of values equal those of the same workbook as `.xlsx` |
| Damaged and deliberately broken files, plus 5,000 generated mutations | No crash or hang within 4 GB and 120 seconds |
| Speed: a 100,000-row by 20-column sheet, and the largest public sheet (151 MB) | First screen under 1 and 3 seconds; painting under 8 ms for 95% of frames; an edit repainted within 16 ms for 99% of edits |

Version 1.0 raises the recalculation bar to 98% of workbooks and 99.9% of cells, and the function test files to 95%.

**The workbooks.** The public collections (business models from the Enron case, school and lab workbooks, and workbooks from government sites) are split in two by each workbook's own name, so that copies of one workbook stay together: a practice half for tuning, and a held-out half that is only ever scored, in totals. The LibreOffice and POI test files are never tuned on; they are used for crash and hang sweeps.

## The model

- `libs/base/calc`: values (number, text, true/false, error, empty, and arrays from the start), number formats and dates (both date systems), the formula tokenizer and parser to bytecode, the evaluator over an abstract cell source, the dependency graph, and the function registry. No GUI.
- `libs/office/sheet`: the workbook model (sparse cells in row blocks, shared strings, styles, merges, names, tables, and the parts kept as read), the `.xlsx` reader and writer, then `.xls` and `.ods`.
- `libs/shell/grid`: the grid view on a grid model. Row heights are a default plus overrides with running sums; only the cells in view are painted, from a cache of their display text.
- `apps/crunch`: the window, commands, undo, sort, filter, and pages for printing.

**Recalculation is incremental.** A cell lists the cells that depend on it; a range is kept as a range in an index per sheet, never expanded to its cells. An edit marks what depends on it and recalculates in order with an explicit stack (a running balance 100,000 rows deep would overflow the system's stack). Volatile functions (`NOW`, `RAND`, `INDIRECT`, `OFFSET`) are re-registered on each run. Recalculation runs on a worker in short slices, so typing never waits, and Esc stops it; the window puts an edited cell in with the worker held back for a moment, and the worker finds what reads it and works it out. Loading, saving, AutoRecover, sorting, and CSV parsing run on worker threads from a snapshot. The arithmetic follows the format's established behavior, oddities included (February 29, 1900 exists; sums that should be zero come out as zero), because the results files store depend on it.

**Undo** keeps changes, not copies: a command records the cells (content, formula, and format, as they were and as they are), styles, sort order, or inserted rows it changed, and undo puts the cells back and the worker works out what depends on them again.

**Hostile files.** Every count, size, and depth a file controls is bounded, at least ten times above what real workbooks use, and formula nesting is limited. The package reader inflates a part only when it is read, and sheets are read by a streaming scanner, so one huge sheet never has to fit in memory at once.

## Waves

Each wave ends with its own gate: the tests, the layer check, and the yardstick numbers it moves.

1. **The yardstick and the readers it needs.** `jcrunchtool`, which runs a list of workbooks under memory and time limits and writes a table of results; the package reader inflating parts only when read; a streaming sheet reader with shared strings; and the first scores (opening, cell counts, the formulas' stored results read).
2. **Number formats.** The whole format language (sections and conditions, colors, text, fractions, elapsed time, both date systems, locales), checked against stored formatted results.
3. **Formulas.** The tokenizer, parser, and evaluator, the dependency graph, and the first 110 functions, scored by recalculating every formula. Wave 3a is the language, the evaluator, the functions, and the score that works each formula out once from the values the file stored; wave 3b is the dependency graph, the calculation in dependency order (with circles, slices, and edits), and the score that works every formula out from the constants.
4. **The grid and editing.** The window, the grid, selection, editing, undo, formatting, and saving `.xlsx`, scored for speed and for saving. Wave 4a is the first window: it opens workbooks and shows them (the grid, the selection, the numbers of a selection, the sheet tabs, the formula bar, and the calculation after the load), scored for speed. Wave 4b is editing, undo, formatting, and saving `.xlsx`: 4b-1 is typing, Delete, undo, and saving `.xlsx` and `.xlsm` (with the yardstick for saving), 4b-2a inserting and deleting rows, columns, and cells, the sizes of columns and rows, the clipboard, fill, AutoSum, and rules of validation (with the yardstick for the edits that move cells), 4b-2b numbers in the person's language, conditional formats shown, the formatting commands, sheets, default sizes, and the display lifts, and the waves after it Find and Replace, sort and filter, and printing.
5. **Toward the preview.** `.xls` and `.ods` reading, printing, charts that follow their cells, and the remaining preview items in the checklist, then the hostile sweep and the held-out scores.

## Where it stands

Wave 1 is built: the readers and the yardstick, on the practice half of the public workbooks (8,371) and no further.

- **Opening.** `jcrunchtool scan` ran every practice workbook in a child process with 120 seconds and 4 GB: all 8,371 opened, none crashed or timed out, and none was read only in part. The slowest is the workbook of the 151 MB sheet (two sheets of 151 MB of XML and 14.8 million cells in all) at 22 seconds and 236 MB; every other workbook took under 4 seconds and 45 MB. The 730 files kept for crash and hang sweeps (LibreOffice and POI test files, damaged and hostile ones among them) gave 0 crashes and 0 hangs, 706 opened, 24 refused with a message (not a usable package).
- **Reading.** Cells, formula cells, and formula cells with a stored result were counted again by a separate program for 500 workbooks, and 477,554 cells of shared formulas (200 workbooks) were expanded again by a second implementation: no difference. Of 9,116,886 formula cells, every one has a result stored, so the recalculation score in wave 3 has an answer to check each formula against. The functions are in the table `jcrunchtool scan` writes; the top 25 are IF, SUM, VLOOKUP, INDEX, MONTH, YEAR, MATCH, MAX, ISNUMBER, FIND, AVERAGE, LEFT, WEEKDAY, AND, OFFSET, SQRT, OR, EXP, MIN, RAND, ABS, LN, SUMIF, HOUR, and ROUND.
- **Memory and limits.** A package keeps each part compressed until it is asked for (`Package::readLazy`), a part is inflated as it is read (`openPart` streams, so a part of any size costs no memory), and a sheet is read in pieces into 24-byte cells, so memory follows the cells and not the XML. Each limit a file controls is at least ten times the largest found in the practice workbooks, and is noted where it is set (`libs/office/sheet/sheetreader.h`): the largest part (2 GB, against 151 MB), everything a package may inflate over its life (4 GB, against 302 MB; a part asked for again and again is counted again), and what one sheet takes, counted at what each thing costs as measured against the memory the process held (2.2 GB, against 207 MB for the largest sheet). A sheet whose cells all carry text, or wait for a shared formula, is refused at the budget with the process near 1.9 GB.
- **Hostile files.** The XML a part holds is passed through a guard before the parser has it (`libs/office/office/xmlstream.h`): it must be UTF-8 (a parser reads UTF-16 as other bytes than a byte count does), well formed where parsers disagree, with no DOCTYPE, no element of more than 1,000 attributes, and no piece (a text, a tag with its attributes, a comment) of more than 1.6 MB, so the parser never holds more than that at once. A workbook whose sheets share a part is refused. These are tested with 200 MB texts, comments, and attributes, 40,000 attributes in UTF-16, and a workbook of 40 sheets that are one part.
- **Speed.** The parser (`QXmlStreamReader`) takes about three quarters of the time, and the whole reads about 18 MB of XML a second: a sheet of 151 MB takes about 9 seconds. The first-screen target of wave 4 is met by stopping the read after the first rows, which the reader allows.

For wave 3: the text of a shared formula for each of its cells is worked out when asked for, by moving its references; it costs about half a microsecond for each reference (measured on 50,829 formulas with 592,424 references), so asking for every formula of a sheet at the bound of 100 million characters takes about 4 seconds. The calculation should work from the master's parsed form and move references in it, not take the text of each cell.

Wave 2 is built: the number formats, in `libs/base/calc` (no GUI; the date code of wave 1 moved there too). Checked on the practice half and no further.

- **The engine** (`calc/numfmt.h`) turns a value and a format code into the text a person sees, the color to draw it in (a number of the 56-color palette, with its RGB), where a `*x` fill goes, and where each `_x` space is. It reads the whole language: up to four sections (positive, negative, zero, text) with the defaults when there are fewer, conditions (`[>100]`, `[<=-5]`), quoted and escaped text, `0 # ?`, the point, groups of three, a division by 1,000 for each comma after the digits, percent, scientific and engineering notation, fractions (up to nine places, fixed denominators), dates and times (`m` is minutes after `h` and before `s`; fractions of a second; `AM/PM` and `A/P`), elapsed `[h] [mm] [ss]`, both date systems (February 29, 1900 and day 0 are there), `[$text-LCID]` currency and locale codes, the system long date and time (`[$-F800]`, `[$-F400]`), `General`, and the built-in formats by id (0 to 49, and the East Asian ones 27 to 36 and 50 to 58). A value is rounded as the original does, from its 15 significant digits as decimal text, rounded once from the number's exact value and half up (2.675 is 2.68 with `0.00`; a value of 16 digits shows zeros after the 15th); time is rounded to the least unit the code shows and the day moves on. General is the 15 digits, in scientific notation from 1E+15 and under 0.0001; given the width of a column, it shows the most digits that fit. Month and day names follow the code's locale: English (every English locale), German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, and Dutch are known by name, any other locale gets English names, and separators of numbers never change with a locale. `formatTextFunction`, `formatFixed`, `formatDollar`, and `dateValueOf` (month first, year first, or with a month's name, and a time after it that is ignored) are the engine's side of `TEXT`, `FIXED`, `DOLLAR`, and `DATEVALUE`. Where the format does not say, these rules were chosen and are in `numfmt.h`: the system's long date or time (`[$-F800]`, `[$-x-sysdate]`) is the pattern written after it if there is one (a file stores the pattern the style had), else the United States' pattern; a section of text that comes last (`m/d/yy;@`) takes no numbers, so one section before it takes all, with a minus sign; a value that no section takes (`[>100]0.0` with 50) can't be shown; a fraction with no whole part is the best fraction of the whole number at every size.
- **Bounds.** A code of more than 4,096 characters (the format allows 255, 233 found), a fifth section, more than 255 places in a section, more than 16 brackets, a fraction of more than nine places, or a code with a quote, bracket, or escape never closed is not valid and its values show as General; no text is made of more than 32,767 characters (a cell holds that many); FIXED and DOLLAR take at most 127 decimals. These are tested with a code of a million characters, 100,000 semicolons, 1,000 brackets, a text repeated 4,000 times over 30,000 characters, and random strings of the characters a code is made of (6,000 in the suite and 360,000 in one long run: no crash, no loop, nothing past the bound).
- **The readers** now have the styles part (`Workbook::styles()`: the codes the file defines, and the format of each cell format, with the built-in ids resolved) and the defined names (`WorkbookInfo::definedNames`). Limits, at least ten times above the practice workbooks: 100,000 number formats (1,736 found), 1,000,000 cell formats (1,887), 4,000,000 characters of codes, 100,000 names (3,295), and 20,000,000 characters of names and what they stand for.
- **The yardstick** is `jcrunchtool formats`. A formula that is `TEXT(x, "code")`, `FIXED(x, n, no_commas)`, or `DOLLAR(x, n)` of a number or of one cell (of its sheet, another's by name, or a defined name for one cell), alone or joined with other text by `&` or `CONCATENATE` (cells and strings joined as they are), has the text a format should make of x stored beside it; each is a case, formatted here and compared with the stored text. In the 8,371 practice workbooks 238 formulas are such cases (in 25 workbooks: 235 of `TEXT`, 3 of `FIXED`, none of `DOLLAR`; 16 distinct pairs of function and code, 149 of them `TEXT` with `00`), and 238 match. That is fewer than the 500 the bar would like, because most of the 8,920 formulas that call `TEXT` are inside another function (`DATEVALUE`, `IF`, `UPPER`) or take a number that a formula works out, which waits for wave 3. So two more kinds of case are counted apart: `DATEVALUE(TEXT(x, "mm/dd/yy"))`, whose day number is stored (6,353, all match: the text reads back as the day it was), and a number joined to text with no call (65,910, all match: the text General makes, though 57,001 of them are zero and none is under 0.0001 or over 1E+15, so the switch to scientific notation is checked by tests and not by the files). The whole run takes 108 seconds. The 730 files kept for crash and hang sweeps gave 0 crashes and 0 hangs.
- **The codes cells use.** The 516 distinct number formats of the practice workbooks all parse. The most used, by cells with a value: `General` (21.5 million), `#,##0 ;[Red](#,##0)`, `_(* #,##0_);_(* \(#,##0\);_(* "-"??_);_(@_)`, `#,##0`, `_(* #,##0.00_);...`, `_("$"* #,##0.00_);...`, `0.00`, `m/d/yyyy`, `0%`, and `"$"#,##0_);[Red]\("$"#,##0\)`.

Known limits of the built-in ids: ids 59 to 81 (which systems of other languages assign) are General here, ids 27 to 36 and 50 to 58 (the East Asian date and time forms) print a Gregorian year where they mean an era, and ids 5 to 8, 42, and 44 (with a `$`) and 14 and 22 (`m/d/yyyy`) are the United States' forms, which another system shows in its own.

Not yet: the engine is not wired to a grid (wave 4 gives it the width of the column, and caches the text of the cells in view); `TEXT` codes written in another language's letters (the German `TT.MM.JJJJ`), eras and other calendars (`e`, `g`, `b`), `[DBNum]` numerals, and locale-specific separators; merged ranges, tables, and the parts a workbook keeps as read; `.xls` and `.ods`; ZIP64 packages, which every reader here refuses. The held-out half has not been scored (only the lead scores it, in totals).

Waves 3a and 3b are built: formulas, in `libs/base/calc` (no GUI); the calculation of a workbook, in `libs/office/sheet/calculation.h`, with the dependency graph in `libs/base/calc/graph.h`; and two yardsticks in `libs/office/sheet/recalc.h`, the check of each formula on its own from the values the file stored (wave 3a), and the full recalculation from the constants (wave 3b). Checked on the practice half and no further.

- **The language** (`calc/formula.h`). A formula as a file stores it is parsed to a short list of postfix instructions, so a chain of any length (`A1+A2+...`) runs without recursion. It reads numbers, texts, `TRUE` and `FALSE`, and errors; references in A1 form (`$` marks, areas, whole rows and columns, other sheets by name or in quotes, `Sheet1:Sheet3!A1` through several sheets, `[1]Sheet1!A1` in another workbook) and in R1C1 form when asked; defined names of the workbook and of a sheet; references to a table's columns, parsed and kept but not worked out; array constants; calls with the prefixes `_xlfn.` and `_xlws.` and with arguments left out; and the operators in the order the files depend on: the range, intersection, and union operators, a minus sign in front, `%`, `^`, `* /`, `+ -`, `&`, and the comparisons, each grouping from the left (so `-2^2` is 4 and `2^3^2` is 64). References are kept as written for the cell the text was written for, so the cells of a shared formula share one parsed program and move its references as they run, as wave 1 asked. Bounds: 32,768 characters, 128 levels of nesting, 1,000 arguments to a call, 100,000 cells in an array constant, 65,536 instructions; random text of the characters a formula is made of (40,000 strings in the suite) never crashes or loops.
- **The evaluator** (`calc/eval.h`) runs a program over an abstract `CellSource`, which the workbook model (`WorkbookSource`) and the tests' grid both implement. A value is a number, text, true or false, an error, empty, or an array. A text is read as a number where a number is needed (`"1,000"`, `"$5"`, `"5%"`, `"(5)"`, dates and times); an empty cell is 0 or `""`; numbers sort before text and text before true and false, text without regard to case; an error passes through, the leftmost first; two numbers that agree to 15 digits are equal. A formula for one value takes the cell that a range crosses (implicit intersection) and the first of an array; an array formula, and the arguments of functions that take arrays (`SUMPRODUCT`, `INDEX`, `LOOKUP`), work on whole arrays, with a function of one value made for each element. `IF`, `IFERROR`, `IFNA`, and `CHOOSE` work out only the argument they need. One evaluation takes at most 50 million steps (a cell read is a step; an array of a million cells is half a million, since building it takes that long; comparing texts is a step for every 32 characters that agree, so a column of long texts matched against a long text runs out of steps), builds no array of more than 2 million cells and has no more than 6 million alive at once (56 bytes each: a formula holds about 340 megabytes of arrays at the most, and the arrays that would pass it are refused as they are asked for, not when 255 of them are built), makes no more than 64 million characters of text as the elements of arrays and none of more than 32,767 characters, keeps the texts `INDIRECT` is given, parsed, in at most 16 megabytes, and nests no defined name or `INDIRECT` more than 16 deep. A name that stands for itself, directly or through other names, is met once and gives `#REF!` (it is not worked out again inside itself). Past a bound the evaluation stops and says which. `NOW` and `TODAY` read the clock once for an evaluation. A caller that works out many formulas gives them all one reading through `EvalContext::now` (from `clockSerial`), so that every `NOW` among them is one moment (the yardstick fixes it at noon on March 15, 2024); with no reading given, each evaluation reads the clock for itself. A month and day alone (`"1/2"`) are that day of the year of the clock the evaluation reads.
- **The functions** (`calc/functions.h`): 206, in a registry that gives each its arguments and what each argument is (a value, a reference or array kept as it is, one worked out as an array), and marks `NOW`, `TODAY`, `RAND`, `RANDBETWEEN`, `OFFSET`, and `INDIRECT` as volatile. `INDIRECT` takes a text that names a reference (a cell, a range, a name) and is `#REF!` for a formula's text (`OFFSET(A1,1,0)`); R1C1 text means what it says for the cell it is worked out in. Of the 75 most used, every one that a workbook does not define for itself is there but `XIRR` and `DSUM`; `TEXT`, `FIXED`, and `DOLLAR` go through wave 2's engine. A function newer than the first release counts only when the file writes it with its `_xlfn.` prefix (`CONCAT` without one is some other function). The database functions (`DSUM`, `DAVERAGE`, `DCOUNT`, `DCOUNTA`, `DMAX`, `DMIN`, `DGET`, `DPRODUCT`, `DSTDEV`, `DSTDEVP`, `DVAR`, `DVARP`; a text condition with no operator matches the texts that begin with it), `DDB`, `VDB`, `XNPV`, and the rates `IRR` and `XIRR` are in. `jcrunchtool functions` lists them.
- **Rules the files showed.** Worked out by comparing with the stored results, and each tested: a power with a whole exponent is made by squaring and multiplying, any other as `exp(y ln x)` in doubles (a correctly rounded `pow` differs in the last digits in about one cell of every few thousand); a difference of numbers that cancel is zero when what is left is within 2^-49 of the larger term and the subtraction or addition is the last operation of the formula (9450 less 9449.999999999989 is 0, but 0.01 less 0.00999999999999997 is not), and `SUM` does the same at each step within 2^-50; `SUBTOTAL` leaves out cells that are `SUBTOTAL`s; `EOMONTH` and `EDATE` count February 1900 as the calendar does; a lookup compares numbers exactly while `=` compares to 15 digits; an empty third argument of `MATCH` is 0 and an empty last argument of `VLOOKUP` is `FALSE`; the empty cells of an array show as 0; a defined name's moving references come round the edge of the sheet; a text that is the only value of a `SUMPRODUCT` argument is `#VALUE!`; the loan functions are worked out in the widest type the platform has; `DAYS360` follows the rule of the NASD as it is stated (the 30th of a month of 30 days, and the 28th of February, stay as they are unless the start is the end of February too) and the european one; `YEARFRAC` by actual days divides by the length of the year the span is in, 366 for a span of a year or less that holds a February 29, and for a longer span the average of the years it touches; the weeks of `WEEKNUM` type 21 and `ISOWEEKNUM` belong to the year of their Thursday, in either date system.
- **The dependency graph** (`calc/graph.h`, `calc/refs.h`). A formula is a node. What it reads is told to the graph as single cells and as blocks, which are never expanded to their cells. Each sheet has a tree over its 16,384 columns, and a block is put in the few nodes of it that together are its columns (one node for a single column or a whole row, at most 28 for any block); a node holds the intervals of rows told to it in sorted runs that are merged as they grow, so that putting one in costs little and asking costs the logarithm of what the node holds, at any size. Asked what reads a cell, or a block, the graph names the formulas by looking at the nodes that hold the cell, not at every formula (the tests count the items a query looks at: with 600,000 cells told, all in one column, it is under two hundred at the most, and the 48 blocks of twelve columns that hold a cell, among twenty thousand that share their rows, are found by looking at about a hundred items, not at all of them). A formula that is told again what it reads (one that reads where its values say: `INDIRECT`, `OFFSET`, a defined name, the range operator over references that functions give as in `A1:INDEX(C:C,3)`, and `SUMIF` and `AVERAGEIF` with a sum range of the shape of the range) drops what it read before at once, for however many cells that was, by a number it carries, and costs nothing if what it tells is what it told; the graph lets go of what was dropped, in one pass, when it comes to as much as is held, so that a thousand formulas that tell the same block and a cell that moves, sixty times, hold what they read now. The graph takes at most 512 MiB (about twelve million single cells, or a few million blocks of eight columns; `CalcOptions::maxGraphBytes`): past it a formula is not tracked and is worked out in every recalculation, with the same values, and a graph that is full says no at once, and does not go over what it holds for each formula it refuses. The references of a shared formula are those of its first cell moved to each cell, from one parsed program. A random test checks the graph against asking every formula (the single cells, blocks, whole columns and rows, and three-dimensional blocks of 1,500 formulas), and another that random edits leave what a whole recalculation gives.
- **The calculation** (`sheet/calculation.h`). Every formula cell is a node, with its result held beside the cells (a node is 32 bytes). A recalculation works the formulas that are dirty out in the order they depend on each other, with an explicit stack: a formula that reads a cell not yet worked out is put to wait for it (what it read that was not ready is found while it is worked out, so a block of a thousand formulas not yet worked out is waited for at once), so that a running balance of a hundred thousand rows each reading the one below it needs no deeper a call stack than one row; a formula that waits is worked out again after what it waited for (twice in all, unless what it reads depends on what it finds: forty nested `INDIRECT` are worked out once more for each level, which the bound on the steps of a recalculation, below, holds in). Formulas that wait for each other are found as the work goes, as the groups of the depth-first search that the stack of the work is (Tarjan's way), in time in proportion to their number: a circle of a million formulas, one of them reading a block that holds all the others, is found in two seconds. A group is given a second try, each formula worked out again with what is worked out by then, and the formulas that wait again are in a circle, every one of the group: a formula that reads itself, directly or round a circle, is circular, and its result is 0. So a formula that reads a cell only when another is empty is in no circle when the other holds something, and a block that holds the formula that reads it is a circle however the edits came, and every formula of overlapping circles is marked; a formula that reads through a name, `INDIRECT`, or `OFFSET` tells the graph what it read each time it is worked out, finished or not, so that an edit that breaks a circle through a name reaches the formula that closed it. In the check against the files a circle is kept apart with the value the file has, since what a file stores for one is what the last calculation left (0 for 1,271 of the 8,532 circular cells of the practice half). What a formula does (volatile, reads where its values say) follows the defined names it uses, and the names those use: a name defined as `RAND()` makes a formula that adds it to a cell volatile. A recalculation takes a billion steps at the most in all its slices (`CalcOptions::recalcWorkLimit`; the busiest workbook of the practice half takes 149 million): a cell of an array that a formula builds counts five steps, and the steps and comparisons of the walk over the rows of a sheet to read a column count a quarter of a step each; when the steps are taken the formulas not yet worked out have the error `#VALUE!` (kept apart, the file's value, of the class past the bounds), the recalculation is over, and the next, after an edit, has the whole bound again. The texts and arrays that formulas gave are held to 512 MiB for the workbook (`CalcOptions::maxHeldBytes`), and an array formula holds the cells of its range (a range of one cell holds one value, not the million cells its formula may make): a formula whose value would pass it has the error. The clock is read once for a recalculation. A recalculation can be done in slices of a given work and stopped (`recalculate(budget, cancel)`); what was not reached is still to be worked out. An edit (`setValue`, `setFormula`, `clearCell`) marks the formulas that read the cell, and those that read them, and nothing else: the work of the next recalculation is those formulas (a test counts them, in a workbook of 7,000 formulas, after edits to a cell that three of them depend on, and to the end of a chain of a hundred thousand). The formulas that change by themselves, and those that read where their values say, are worked out in every recalculation, as the original does.
- **The full-recalculation yardstick** is `jcrunchtool recalc-full <keys.txt> <out-dir>`: each workbook is read, every formula worked out from its constants (the results the file stored are read for nothing but the comparison), in dependency order, and each compared with its stored result as `recalc` does. A row hidden by hand and a row hidden by the filter are two flags of `RowSpec` (`hand`, `filtered`; `hidden` is either). The file does not tell them apart, so a hidden row inside a filter that has conditions is read as the filter's. The filter is `Sheet::autoFilter`, read whole (up to 200,000 listed values a sheet), written back only when a person changed it (the element, `filterMode`, the colors as differential formats, and the hidden name `_xlnm._FilterDatabase`), and moved by inserted and deleted rows and columns like a merged range.   - **How an edit reaches the worker (the design of wave 4a, adjusted, and why).** The plan was an edit sent to the worker, which puts it in the cells. But the window draws from the cells directly, and walks them for copying, the numbers of a selection, and Ctrl and an arrow; were only the worker to change them, the window could read a block of a row as the worker moved the cells after the new one in it. So the window puts the cell in itself, with the worker held back for the moment (`WorkbookLoader::hold`): it sets the flag that the evaluator and each step of the slice look at, then takes the lock the worker has a slice at a time, which the worker lets go of at its next chunk. The window changes the cell and its place in the cells (`Calculation::put`, with `Marking::Later`: moving the cell in its block of rows, 0.15 to 0.2 ms), and lets go. Finding what reads the cell, marking it, and working it out stay the worker's, and its results come back in batches as before. The worker no longer ends when the formulas are worked out: it waits, and works out what the window changes, F9, and what Esc left. An edit made after Esc takes up what was left. A save asked for during a calculation waits for it, so that the file has the formulas' results. While the workbook is being read, or if a sheet could not be read whole, or while a save is going on, nothing can be changed (the person is told why).
  - **Two bounds the edit made visible.** The step that finds the formulas that read the cells that changed ignored the answer of its chunk check, so a slice never ended there: marking the readers of a cell that 200,000 formulas read took one slice of some 20 ms, and the window waited for that long to change a cell (up to 13 ms). It honors the answer now (a counted test fails without it), a stop flag ends a step at its next chunk, and the graph's search looks at the flag as it goes (every 4,096 formulas found; a search that is stopped is made again, whole, when the calculation goes on). The window now waits about a millisecond at most for the worker to let go, with the 200,000-reader cell included.

  - **What it is.** Carried from 4b-1: the translation tool finds the messages of `sheetreader.cpp` and `stats.cpp` (a scratch `lupdate` outside the repository lists them; the calculation's messages are wrapped in `QT_TRANSLATE_NOOP` so that it can), and a renamed column of a table is renamed in the defined names, rules of validation, conditional formats, and charts that name it.   - **Inserting and deleting: the design and why.** The calculation does it at once (`Calculation::applyEdit`, `calcedit.cpp`; a `StructEdit` says what, and an `EditRecord` holds what Undo needs). The cells are shifted in one pass over the blocks from the first row (`CellStore::shiftRows`; columns, `shiftColumns`; cells shifted along a few rows or columns use the older `reshuffle`), so the cost is the cells that move (some 40 ns each for rows, which are made into blocks again, and some 12 for columns, which are moved where they are), not the sheet. The formulas that read what moves are found by the graph (its readers of the area that moves, and the list of the formulas that are told where they read or are kept apart), and each is written again with `adjustFormula` (for rows, the graph is told at once where everything it holds is now, `shiftRows`, and a formula whose text only moved keeps its node); a reference to a cell that is deleted becomes `#REF!`. For the usual formula, one of plain cell references, numbers, and operators, a fast way reads the text directly, not through the lexer: a test makes thousands of formulas and edits and compares both ways. A range grows or shrinks with the rows inside it; a whole-column or whole-row reference is left alone. Names, rules of validation, merged ranges, tables (`tableAfter` says where one is after the edit: it moves, grows, shrinks, or the edit is refused), and the cells of a cut follow too. Each field is read as it would be typed in the person's locale (`readEntry`): `1.234,5` in German is a number, `12%` a percent shown as typed, `$5` an amount of money, `3/15/2024` or `15.03.2024` a date, `TRUE` a boolean (and the word of the person's language: `WAHR` and `FALSCH`, `VRAI` and `FAUX`, and so on for some twenty languages, in any case), `#N/A` an error, and a field that begins with `=` is a formula (worked out after loading). The chosen departures from the common program, which keep what a number or a date would lose: a field of digits that begins with a zero (`007`) or has more than 15 digits stays text, a day and a month with no year stay text in every form, order, and language (`1-2`, `3/4`, `12.5`, `March 1`, `Dec 25`; a date with a year, `3/15/2024`, `1-Mar-24`, `March 2024`, is a date, in the person's order), because the year would have to be made up, and so does the gene `MARCH1`, and a field that begins with an apostrophe is kept as it is. The sheet's limits are enforced: what is past row 1,048,576 or column 16,384 or the 32,767 characters of a cell is not read, and the sheet says so (so nothing can be saved over a file that was cut: the same as for any sheet not read whole). The file is scanned in pieces of a million characters, the first rows (the first screen) are read at once, and a field the word list cannot make a value of (any text with a word of its own in it) skips the slow reading of numbers and dates. An empty file is an empty sheet.
- **Saving.** A workbook read from `.ods`, `.fods`, `.csv`, `.tsv`, or `.txt` is saved as an `.xlsx` written from the model, as for `.xls` (`xlsxbase.h`): the person is told in plain words what the file was, that it is saved as `.xlsx` under a new name, and that the file is not changed; the name offered is the file's with `.xlsx`, the file itself is never the answer (`Workbook::isConverted()` is the one question the save code asks), and later saves patch the `.xlsx`. The file is found by where it really is (links followed; capitals ignored where the system ignores them) and guarded for the whole session, also after the first save moves the window's file to the `.xlsx`. **Save As offers `.csv`** (for a workbook of any form): the values of the sheet in view, as the cells show them, in UTF-8 with the byte order mark (so that the common program reads accented letters right), a semicolon between them where the language writes a comma in its numbers and a comma elsewhere, a text in quotes when it holds the separator, a quote, or a line break, every row up to the last one with something (hidden ones too), rows ended by CRLF (the separator is the region's list separator on Windows, else the semicolon where the language writes a comma in its numbers); the person is told first that only this sheet's values are written, and that the sheet was read only in part when it was; a name chosen with the `.csv` kind of file and no ending typed gets `.csv`; a `.csv` written from the question asked when the window is closed is the answer to it and the window closes, and a failed one leaves the window open; it is done in slices of 4 milliseconds on the window's thread from a model of its own (changing sheets meanwhile changes nothing), nothing can be edited meanwhile, the file takes its name when whole, and the workbook is not marked as saved by it.

- **What is read and drawn.** A sheet's drawing part (the one its relationships name; a chart sheet has one too) is read anchor by anchor: two-cell anchors (the usual: the object runs from one place in a cell to another and moves and sizes with the cells), one-cell anchors (a place and a size), and absolute ones (a place of the sheet), each with its `editAs`, and each holding a chart, a picture (with its crop), a shape or text box, a group, or a connector; an anchor a newer program wrapped for older ones is read in the branch that holds an object. The reader only finds the objects (`libs/office/sheet/drawing.h`): a chart is read by the chart reader the presentations use (`libs/office/office/chart.h`) and drawn by its SVG code, and a picture, shape, text box, group, or connector is read by the drawing code the presentations use (`drawingml.h`) and drawn by their renderer, with the object's own frame put in the box its anchor gives, so a drawing looks here as it does in JeffDeck and nothing is drawn a second way. Objects the file hides (the stand-ins of form controls, in the ones looked at) are not drawn. The grid's object layer (`libs/shell/grid/objectlayer.h`, given to the view as an overlay: `overlay.h`) places each by its anchor on the rows and columns the sheet has now (a hidden row has no height, so an object whose rows are all hidden is gone; an offset into a cell is no more than the cell), in each of the four parts of a frozen window as its own coordinates put it, at any zoom and scroll, and keeps a picture of each at the size it is shown at. A paint makes nothing: the picture of an object is made between events, when the object is first on the screen (a chart takes what it needs of the cells on the window's thread and its picture is made as an image on a worker thread, two at a time; a picture, shape, text box, group, or connector is made on the window's thread, in steps of at most eight pictures or eight milliseconds), and for the objects a screenful beyond the window a moment after the grid stops; until a picture is there, an outline stands in the object's place. Which objects are on the screen is found through an index of where they are (a grid of squares of 256 units, each holding the objects that touch it, and a list of the few that touch too many), so a paint, the making of pictures, and a click look at the objects near the window and not at every one of the sheet (counted: with 50,000 objects a screen asks about a few hundred); objects are found by their ids through a table, and a chart sheet's object is the first that is not deleted, kept. A picture is kept until the zoom, the size, or what a chart reads changes. The budget is hard: the pictures held and those being made take at most 192 MB (the least recently shown go first); what is on the screen is made smaller (a half, a quarter...) if it would take more than 60% of that; and no picture has more than 8 million pixels. Rows or columns inserted or deleted, and sizes changed, move the objects where they are and leave their pictures (and the one picked) alone: an object whose size the edit does not change is not drawn again, and a chart is drawn again only if what it reads is no longer what it was drawn from (a chart whose cells only moved with it is the same picture). An object that keeps its size when cells are resized (`editAs` "oneCell" or "absolute") is measured when the sheet is whole, keeps it as rows and columns change, and is saved so.
